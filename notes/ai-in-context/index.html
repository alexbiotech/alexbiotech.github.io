AI in context: branches that meet

I want to understand AI along two axes. Looking across the present, I can compare neighboring approaches and ask where their ideas meet. Looking backward, I can follow a problem to its origins and see why different branches formed. That gives me a better basis for thinking about what might change next.

Different beginnings

Mathematics and statistics supplied a long forehistory: logic, probability, optimization, and estimation from observations. Least squares was published by Legendre in 1805 and developed by Gauss in 1809. These questions predate AI as a named field.

The 1956 Dartmouth meeting helped establish that field. Symbolic approaches pursued reasoning through explicit representations and rules. In a parallel line, Rosenblatt’s 1958 perceptron learned classification through changing connection weights. One branch emphasized describing knowledge; another emphasized learning from examples.

Later branches offered different answers to prediction. CART (1984) organized decisions through successive partitions. Support-vector networks (1995) and kernel methods connected classification with geometry and statistical learning; random forests (2001) combined trees. These landmarks came after the perceptron and developed alongside neural approaches.

Representation, scale, and feedback

The 1986 backpropagation paper by Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams helped establish learning internal representations in multilayer networks. The 2012 ImageNet result brought deep visual learning together with large datasets and GPU training. The 2017 Transformer offered an attention-based architecture for sequence modeling.

Scaling laws for language models, studied by Kaplan and colleagues in 2020, related prediction loss to model size, data, and training compute. They describe empirical relationships within studied regimes. Whether those relationships carry into a new task remains a separate question.

Reinforcement learning has its own history of learning action from rewards; DQN (2015) connected it with deep representations. Diffusion generation developed through another branch, including Sohl-Dickstein and colleagues’ 2015 work and DDPM (2020). Architecture, generative process, and feedback describe different aspects of a system, so these branches can meet.

A cross-section of the present

Prediction and statistical learning

Regression, trees, kernels, and neural networks connect observations with predicted outcomes. Their assumptions and representations differ.

Deep networks learn representations; Transformer architectures and scaling studies address different parts of their development.

Generation and design

Language generation and diffusion connect learned patterns with new candidates. Protein design is one meeting point with biology.

Action and feedback

Reinforcement learning studies sequential decisions. Agent systems also bring in planning, memory, and external feedback.

Where I want to follow the connections

RFdiffusion (2023) is a concrete historical crossing from diffusion into protein design. The biology note follows the older distinction between predicting a structure and designing something new.

For the immune-regulation questions I hope to pursue in postdoctoral research, I want to understand how AI’s progress depends on biological evidence. If measurements become more informative and comparable, more questions may become tractable. If context and feedback remain difficult to capture, a larger model may leave the central uncertainty unresolved. Which missing observation would most change the explanation?
