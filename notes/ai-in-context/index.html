AI in context: where different threads meet

I like looking at AI along two axes. Horizontally, I want to compare neighboring methods side by side and see where their ideas overlap. Vertically, I want to trace a problem back to its roots and see why the field split into different camps in the first place. Having both views makes it much easier to think clearly about what might change next.

Different starting points

Long before anyone called a field “AI,” math and statistics were already building the toolkit: logic, probability, optimization, and fitting models to noisy observations. Legendre published least squares in 1805, and Gauss developed it further in 1809. The core questions are much older than the name.

The 1956 Dartmouth workshop helped put the field on the map. Early symbolic AI tried to build reasoning out of explicit representations and rules. Running in parallel, Rosenblatt’s 1958 perceptron learned to classify by adjusting connection weights. One camp focused on writing down knowledge; the other focused on learning from examples.

Later on, other branches came up with their own takes on prediction. CART (1984) broke decisions down through step-by-step splits. Support-vector networks (1995) and kernel methods tied classification to geometry and statistical learning theory; random forests (2001) pooled ensembles of trees. All of these arrived after the perceptron and grew up right alongside neural networks.

Representation, scale, and feedback

In 1986, Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams popularized backpropagation, showing how multilayer networks could learn internal representations on their own. In 2012, the ImageNet breakthrough brought deep vision together with large datasets and GPUs. Then the 2017 Transformer introduced an attention-based architecture that reshaped sequence modeling.

In 2020, Kaplan and colleagues mapped out scaling laws for language models, tracking how prediction loss drops with model size, data, and training compute. Those are empirical patterns inside the regimes people tested. Whether they hold in a new domain is a separate question.

Reinforcement learning, which learns how to act from rewards, grew along its own track until DQN (2015) wired it into deep networks. Diffusion models came out of yet another lineage, from Sohl-Dickstein and colleagues in 2015 to DDPM in 2020. Because architecture, generative process, and feedback describe different parts of a system, these branches don’t have to stay separate; they can combine.

A cross-section of today

Prediction and statistical learning

Regression, trees, kernels, and neural networks all map observations to predictions, each with different assumptions and representations.

Representations and scale

Deep networks learn their own features; Transformers and scaling laws each explain a different slice of why they took off.

Generation and design

Language models and diffusion turn learned patterns into new candidates. Protein design is one place where that meets biology head-on.

Action and feedback

Reinforcement learning focuses on sequential decisions; agent systems add planning, memory, and feedback from the outside world.

Where I want to follow the trail

RFdiffusion (2023) is a concrete example of diffusion crossing straight into protein design. My biology note picks up the older split between predicting an existing structure and designing something new.

For the immune-regulation work I want to do as a postdoc, the big question for me is how much AI’s progress depends on biological evidence. If measurements get sharper and easier to compare across studies, a lot of hard problems may suddenly open up. If context and feedback stay murky, even a much bigger model will leave the core uncertainty untouched. What’s the one missing measurement that would change the whole story?
