Biology in context: regulation, editing, and design

I once planned a series of notes about how synthetic biology labs arrived at their present questions. A map of today’s research directions would show one set of connections; tracing those questions backward would show another. I still want to understand both: what neighboring fields contribute now, and which earlier problems gave rise to them.

A community formed around older questions

Gene regulation already had a conceptual foundation in Jacob and Monod’s 1961 operon model. Artificial switches and oscillators reported in 2000 made memory and rhythm into questions about the behavior of gene circuits. Synthetic Biology 1.0, held at MIT in 2004, brought biology, engineering, and computation into an organized research community. The meeting marked a convergence of existing work and shared ambitions.

The double helix gave DNA a structural model; deciphering the genetic code explained how nucleic-acid sequences correspond to amino acids; restriction enzymes made specific sites of DNA cleavage accessible to study. Gene editing asks a related question: what can a change in genetic information tell us about biological function? Before CRISPR-based editing, gene targeting, zinc-finger nucleases (ZFNs), and TALENs had already opened different routes to studying targeted genetic changes. The 2012 work by Jinek, Charpentier, Doudna, and colleagues established programmable RNA-guided DNA cleavage; the 2020 Chemistry Nobel recognized Charpentier and Doudna’s contribution.

Measured structures, inferred relationships, new designs

Protein science brings another set of questions. Kendrew’s team used X-ray crystallography to reveal myoglobin’s three-dimensional structure, an early landmark in determining protein structures. Homology links related proteins through common ancestry; multiple sequence alignments compare related sequences, and patterns of co-evolution can reveal structural constraints. These inferences connect evolutionary biology with structural prediction.

AlphaFold2’s CASP14 result in 2020 and its 2021 paper joined deep learning with structural knowledge and evolutionary information. Prediction asks what structure a protein is likely to have. Design asks what new protein could realize a proposed structure or function. Top7, reported by Baker’s team in 2003, was an earlier de novo design landmark with an experimentally determined structure corresponding to the intended fold.

The 2024 Chemistry Nobel preserved that distinction: half went to David Baker for computational protein design, and half jointly to Demis Hassabis and John Jumper for structure prediction. RFdiffusion (2023) provides a later example of AI crossing into protein design, connecting diffusion research with a longstanding biological design problem.

Where the branches meet today

Regulation and cell behavior

Molecular biology and systems biology connect regulatory relationships with the behavior of cells over time.

Editing and causal explanation

Genetics and gene editing connect changes in genetic information with observed consequences.

Structure and design

Structural biology, evolutionary inference, and AI connect measured forms, predicted forms, and proposed new molecules.

Immune regulation and clinical research

Immunology connects molecular and cellular events with responses unfolding across tissues and time; clinical research adds observations at the level of people.

The questions I want to carry forward

My doctoral work on circular RNA translation control and delivery-vector design leads me toward prospective postdoctoral questions about immune regulation. I want to understand how a response depends on cell state, how its duration changes its meaning, and what explains its ending.

The AI note asks how learning depends on feedback. Here, I want to ask what the feedback represents biologically. If molecular, cellular, and clinical observations can be connected more clearly, which explanations would become testable? Where those links remain uncertain, what kind of knowledge is missing?
