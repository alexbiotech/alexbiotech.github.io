Biology in context: regulation, editing, and design

Why do labs that all call themselves “synthetic biology” work on completely different problems? I find it helps to ask two things: which neighboring fields are they pulling tools from today, and what older questions brought them here in the first place?

Older questions before the name existed

Gene regulation already had a clean conceptual frame in Jacob and Monod’s 1961 operon model. In 2000, engineered toggle switches and oscillators turned cellular memory and rhythm into questions about how gene circuits behave. When MIT hosted Synthetic Biology 1.0 in 2004, it pulled biologists, engineers, and computer scientists into an organized community, gathering work and ambitions that were already underway.

The double helix gave DNA a physical structure; cracking the genetic code showed how nucleotide sequences map to amino acids; restriction enzymes gave biologists a way to cut DNA at specific spots. Gene editing pushes the next logical question: if we change the genetic text, what does that teach us about biological function? Before CRISPR, gene targeting, zinc-finger nucleases (ZFNs), and TALENs had already opened earlier routes to targeted edits. Then Jinek, Charpentier, Doudna, and colleagues showed in 2012 how to program RNA-guided DNA cleavage, work recognized by the 2020 Chemistry Nobel for Charpentier and Doudna.

Measured structures, evolutionary clues, and brand-new designs

Protein science comes at biology from a different angle. Kendrew’s team solved myoglobin’s 3D structure by X-ray crystallography, one of the earliest landmarks in seeing what proteins look like. Homology ties related proteins back to shared ancestors; multiple sequence alignments line those relatives up, and patterns of co-evolution reveal structural constraints. That’s where evolutionary biology feeds straight into structure prediction.

AlphaFold2’s CASP14 result in 2020 and its 2021 paper fused deep learning with structural knowledge and evolutionary signal. Still, prediction and design are different games. Prediction asks what shape an existing protein probably folds into; design asks what brand-new protein could carry out a shape or function you have in mind. In 2003, David Baker’s team built Top7, an early de novo design landmark whose measured crystal structure matched the fold they set out to make.

The 2024 Chemistry Nobel kept that split intact: half went to David Baker for computational protein design, and half jointly to Demis Hassabis and John Jumper for structure prediction. RFdiffusion (2023) is a more recent crossing, bringing AI diffusion models directly into a protein-design problem biologists had been working on for decades.

Where the threads meet today

Regulation and cell behavior

Molecular biology and systems biology link regulatory wiring to how cells behave over time.

Which explanations still hold up when the cellular context changes?

Editing and causation

Genetics and gene editing tie a change in DNA to what you see happen next.

What evidence separates a direct effect from the rest of the system reacting to it?

Structure and design

Structural biology, evolutionary inference, and AI bridge measured structures, predicted structures, and brand-new candidate molecules.

Once you know the structure, what do you still not know about its function?

Immune regulation and clinical research

Immunology connects molecular and cellular events to responses playing out across tissues and time; clinical research brings in what happens in patients.

Which rules stay consistent across all those scales?

The questions I want to carry forward

My PhD work on circular RNA translation control and delivery vectors naturally points me toward immune regulation for my postdoc. I want to understand how a response depends on cell state, how its duration changes what it means biologically, and what brings it to an end.

My AI note asks how learning depends on feedback. In biology, I want to ask what that feedback corresponds to in living tissue. If we can link molecular, cellular, and clinical data more cleanly, which ideas become testable? And where those links still break down, what kind of knowledge are we missing?
