AI4Bio: What happens after the prediction?

I once asked on X whether scaling laws still hold in biology, and went looking for industry examples. Now I think the question needs to be pulled apart. When you add more data, compute, or parameters, which specific tasks get better, why do they improve, and how far do those gains travel?

In biology, context isn’t a footnote; it’s half the problem. Put the same molecule into a different cell state or tissue environment: does the model’s prediction still hold? When it breaks, what is missing: more data, a better description of the conditions, or our own understanding of the underlying biology? Those are different bottlenecks, and I want evidence that separates them.

Which decision does the prediction change?

What I most want to know about AI4Bio is whether it helps researchers make a better call sooner. Finding targets, crunching messy data, repurposing existing drugs, and choosing the next experiment are very different jobs. Lumping them all under “faster drug discovery” hides which part sped up.

If a model flags a new target, how do scientists decide whether it’s worth months of bench work? Faster data analysis only matters if researchers spot the meaningful biological differences sooner. And if a model recommends a new indication or the next experiment, did it change what the team was going to do anyway, and did the follow-up data back it up?

You can’t answer those with one catch-all metric; each task calls for its own kind of proof. A sharper prediction can narrow down the search space. Whether it saves time or leads to a better result depends on what happens once it feeds into a research decision.

Different cases answer different questions

Arc’s one-year look back at Evo 2 includes evaluations on human genetic variants. What I want to know from results like that is what transferable biology the model picked up, and how well its predictions hold in an unfamiliar setting. Overview

OpenAI, PerturbAI, and Scripps Research used Rosalind to comb back through existing mouse brain-cell data and surface leads worth testing. Meanwhile, Isomorphic Labs reported preclinical activity for a molecule picked by IsoDDE. One asks whether AI-driven analysis can uncover a good question; the other asks whether a computational pick holds up in the lab. Neither one tells you yet whether a drug works in patients. Rosalind · IsoDDE

The Virtual Biotech mines historical clinical-trial data to study targets and outcomes, including going back over failed trials. That raises another question I find compelling: can past failures help us revise a broken hypothesis, or point an existing drug toward a new indication worth testing? Paper

How far has the evidence gone?

With industry examples, I try to separate what AI did from what happened to the drug candidate afterward. A 2025 paper on rentosertib reported a 12-week, randomized, placebo-controlled phase 2a trial with 71 participants. That is real human data on the candidate, though larger and longer trials are still needed to settle long-term safety and efficacy. Paper

A case like that makes me want to keep two questions apart: how did the drug perform in the clinic, and what evidence shows that AI made discovery more efficient? The specific questions a case opens up usually teach me more than a headline declaring victory.

Going forward, I want tighter links between predictions, hypotheses, and experimental outcomes: what explanation a prediction points to, what evidence backs it, and why it works in one setting and falls apart in another. That is why scaling interests me at all. Does it help us understand more and make better bets on what to test next?
