I want to build therapies that can read what’s happening in the body or inside a cell, and act only where and when they’re needed. Equally important is how long they stay active, and how they get turned off.

RNA, DNA, and proteins are the building blocks I have in mind. In my PhD, I worked on translation switches controlled by microRNAs, circular-RNA logic that tells cancer cells apart, and protein particles that carry RNA into T cells.

Biology in context: regulation, editing, and design

Cell state and control

What should a system respond to?

Can the signals it reads tell the relevant cell states apart? Does that distinction still hold in a different cell type or tissue environment?

A quick pulse, or a sustained response?

Some therapies only need a brief burst of activity; others need to stay on for a while. I’m interested in how cell state shapes both strength and duration, not only whether a switch flips at the start.

When and how does it stop?

Once the job is done, does the system clear on its own, or do we need a way to shut it down manually? And what lingers afterward?

Immune regulation

I finished my PhD at Tsinghua in 2026 and am joining the School of Biomedical Sciences at HKUMed (The University of Hong Kong) as a postdoctoral fellow. At HKUMed, I’ll study immune regulation and follow clinical research on vaccines, cancer vaccines, and CAR-T therapies. The core question stays the same: when does an immune response help, how long should it last, and when should it stop?

AI4Bio

Does adding more data, compute, or model capacity make predictions hold up across different cell states and conditions? When it does, I want to know why, and whether the resulting hypotheses survive experiments.

I also want to look closely at where AI helps in drug research: finding targets, making sense of complex data, repurposing existing drugs, or picking the next experiment. Each step needs clear evidence: what got faster, and whether it improved researchers’ judgment.
