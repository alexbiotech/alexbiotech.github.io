I want to build therapies that can read what’s happening in the body or inside a cell, and act only where and when they’re needed. Just as important: controlling how long they stay active, and how to turn them off.

RNA, DNA, and proteins are the building blocks I have in mind. During my PhD, I worked on programmable translation control and delivery vectors for circular RNA.

Biology in context: regulation, editing, and design

Cell state and control

What should a system respond to?

Can the signals it reads actually tell the relevant cell states apart? And does that distinction still hold once you move to a different cell type or tissue environment?

A quick pulse, or a sustained response?

Some therapies only need a brief burst of activity; others need to stay on for a while. I’m interested in how cell state shapes both strength and duration—not just whether a switch flips at the start.

When—and how—does it stop?

Once the job is done, does the system clear on its own, or do we need a way to shut it down manually? And what lingers afterward?

Immune regulation

For my postdoc, I want to focus on immune regulation—especially clinical work around vaccines, cancer vaccines, and CAR-T therapies. It’s the same core puzzle in a real clinical setting: when does an immune response help, how long should it last, and when does it need to stop?

AI4Bio

Does adding more data, compute, or model capacity actually make predictions hold up across different cell states and conditions? When it does, why—and do the resulting hypotheses survive real experiments?

I also want to look closely at where AI genuinely helps across drug research: finding targets, making sense of complex data, repurposing existing drugs, or picking the next experiment. Each step needs its own proof—what actually got faster, and did the model help researchers make a better call?
